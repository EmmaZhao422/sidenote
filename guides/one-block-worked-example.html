One study block, start to finish: a worked example

A Sidenote study guide · Published November 21, 2026

Advice about focus is easy to agree with and hard to picture. So here is one ordinary block, written out. The student is made up, and so are her thoughts, but every step is one you can copy tonight. She has a statistics problem set due on Friday and forty-five minutes before dinner.

Before: five minutes

She writes the goal first. Her first version is "work on stats", which gives her nothing to aim at. She changes it to something she can check:

Goal: Problem set 5, questions 1 to 3, written up.

Steps: 1. Re-read the worked example on confidence intervals. 2. Do questions 1 and 2. 3. Do question 3, then check all three against the notes.

Then she empties her head. Two things are already nagging: she owes her landlord a call about the heating, and she is not sure when the lab report is due. Both go on the list before the timer starts, so they don't get to interrupt later. Her phone goes in her bag, on the other side of the room. She spends the last two minutes breathing slowly and looking at step one.

During: forty-five minutes

The timer starts. She has a background sound on, and one box open for stray thoughts. This is what the block looked like from the inside:

  • Minute 4. "Is the heating thing going to cost me money?" It is the landlord again, and it is already on the list. She doesn't write it twice. Back to the worked example.
  • Minute 11. Halfway through question 1: "Why do we divide by n minus 1?" She is curious, and it feels relevant. But answering it now means opening a tab. She types the question in one line and goes on with the formula as given.
  • Minute 17. "I want to check my phone." She types exactly that. Writing it down takes four seconds, and getting up to fetch the phone would take longer.
  • Minute 26. Question 2 goes badly. "Everyone else finds this easy. What if I fail the midterm?" This one stings. She writes it down as it came and puts her pen back on the line where she stopped. It returns a minute later. She doesn't write it again.
  • Minute 38. "A shared sheet of worked examples for the whole study group?" It is a nice idea and has nothing to do with question 3. One line.

When the timer ends she has finished questions 1 and 2 and half of question 3.

After: ten minutes

First she rates the block: 3 out of 5 for focus, plan partly finished. Then she goes through the list, top to bottom, and gives each line an ending.

  • Call the landlord. A to-do. It takes two minutes, and offices close at six. She calls now. Done.
  • When is the lab report due? A question only her course page can answer. She looks: next Wednesday. It goes in her calendar. Done.
  • Why n minus 1? A real question. She writes her guess first ("something about the sample being smaller than the population?"), then looks it up. The short answer: dividing by n would underestimate the spread, because the sample mean sits closer to the sample's own points than the true mean does. Her guess was half right, which is why she will remember it.
  • Check my phone. The block is over, so she can. She sets a ten-minute timer first.
  • What if I fail the midterm? A worry. She asks the only useful question: is there one thing I can do? There is. Question 2 went badly because she doesn't understand one step, so she writes: "Ask about question 2 in office hours on Thursday." The worry has turned into a to-do with a day on it.
  • Shared sheet of worked examples. An idea. Three lines: what it is, who would want it, the cheapest test. The test is one message to the group chat. She sends it.

The half-finished question 3 goes at the top of tomorrow's block.

What the record showed

One block doesn't make a pattern. After a week of them, hers showed three things. Most of her stray thoughts were errands, so she now spends five minutes on admin before she studies. They clustered between minutes 10 and 20, so a wobble at minute 15 no longer feels like a sign that she can't concentrate. And her blocks with rain in the background scored a little higher than her blocks in silence, which she would not have guessed.

She did not get rid of the stray thoughts in that block. All five showed up, and each cost her about five seconds.

Try it

You can do all of this with a sheet of paper and a kitchen timer. Sidenote does the same thing with a timer, a box for the stray thoughts, and the record at the end. If you want the reasoning behind each step, read the five-minute start, the parking lot method and what to do with a worry.
