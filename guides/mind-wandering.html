Why your mind wanders while you study, and what to do instead of fighting it

A Sidenote study guide · Published November 21, 2026

You read a paragraph and reach the end with no idea what it said. Somewhere around the second line you started thinking about dinner, or a message you haven't answered, or whether you chose the right major. It is easy to read this as a personal flaw. It isn't one.

Everyone's mind does this, a lot

In 2010 two Harvard psychologists, Matthew Killingsworth and Daniel Gilbert, used a phone app to interrupt 2,250 people at random moments and ask what they were doing and whether they were thinking about something else. People's minds were elsewhere in 46.9% of the samples. It happened in at least 30% of the samples for every activity they recorded except one (making love).

So a mind that drifts during a chapter on thermodynamics is behaving like a mind. The useful question is what you do in the second after you notice.

Why "just concentrate" doesn't work

When you catch yourself drifting, the usual reaction is to push the thought away and scold yourself. Both cost attention, which is the thing you were short of. And the thought usually comes back, because nothing was done about it. An errand you haven't scheduled is still unscheduled. A question you were curious about is still open.

Three things that work better

  1. Catch it, write one line, go back. The thought gets a place to live that isn't your head. This is the parking lot method, and it is what the box at the bottom of a Sidenote study block is for. It takes about five seconds.
  2. Make the task smaller. Minds leave vague work faster than specific work. "Study chapter 4" gives your attention nothing to hold. "Answer the three end-of-section questions without looking" does. If you drift every few minutes, look at how the task is worded before you blame yourself. The five-minute start is a quick way to do this.
  3. Expect the dip. Many people drift most at a particular point in a session. Once you know yours, a wander at that minute stops feeling like failure. It becomes a cue: write the line, carry on. Sidenote's summary page shows which minute your parked thoughts cluster in.

When wandering is telling you something

Sometimes the drifting has a pattern worth acting on. If you always leave during one subject, the material may be a step too hard or too easy right now, and that is a problem to solve with a tutor or a different resource. If the same worry returns every session, it probably needs a real conversation with someone. And if you are short on sleep, no technique will hold your attention for long.

The same study found people were less happy when their minds wandered than when they stayed on what they were doing. That is one more reason to give stray thoughts somewhere to go: you get the work done, and the session feels better while you do it.

Sources

  • Killingsworth, M. A., & Gilbert, D. T. (2010). A wandering mind is an unhappy mind. Science, 330(6006), 932. doi:10.1126/science.1192439
