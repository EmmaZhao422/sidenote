Five minutes before you study: a short plan beats a long warm-up

A Sidenote study guide

The first ten minutes of a study session are the hardest ones. You open the book, read the same paragraph twice, check the time, and rearrange your desk. Usually the material isn't the problem. You sat down without deciding what, exactly, you were about to do.

A five-minute start fixes that. It isn't a warm-up or a motivational ritual. You make four small decisions before the timer starts, so you don't have to make them while you work.

Minute one: say what this block is for

"Study chemistry" is a mood. "Finish problem set 6, questions 1 to 4" is a goal you can start on. Write one sentence that would let a friend check whether you did it.

Minutes two and three: split it into two or three steps

Steps should be small enough that the first one is obvious. "Re-read the worked example on page 112." "Do questions 1 and 2." "Check answers against the key." If you can't name the first step, you have just found out why you were about to procrastinate, and you found out before the timer started.

There is good evidence that this kind of specific planning helps. The psychologist Peter Gollwitzer calls plans of the form "when X happens, I will do Y" implementation intentions, and his 1999 review found that they make people noticeably more likely to follow through than a general intention to "work hard" does. You can borrow the format: "When the timer starts, I will open to page 112."

Minute four: empty your head

Anything already nagging at you (a message you owe someone, something you need to buy, a worry about tomorrow) will interrupt you later if you leave it loose. Write each one down in a single line now. Don't solve them. Once it is on the page, it stops asking for attention. Our guide to the parking lot method explains why this works.

Minute five: settle

Put the phone out of reach, face down or in another room. Start your background sound if you use one. Take a few slow breaths, with the out-breath longer than the in-breath. Look at your first step. Then begin.

Sidenote runs this routine for you. Before each study block it gives you five minutes: a plan with up to three steps, a place to empty your head, and a breathing guide while the sound fades in. The focus timer starts only after that.

Why not just start?

Sometimes you can. If you already know your next step, skip the routine. The five minutes pay off when you feel resistance, because that resistance is usually vagueness. Once the work has a clear first step, starting is easy.

The routine also helps at the end. When the block is over you can ask a concrete question: did I finish what I planned? Over a few weeks, the answers show you how much you can really do in 25 or 45 minutes, which makes the next plan more realistic.

A version for bad days

If five minutes feels like too much, do the two-line version: one sentence for the goal, one for the first step. Then start a short block, even ten minutes.

Source

  • Gollwitzer, P. M. (1999). Implementation intentions: Strong effects of simple plans. American Psychologist, 54(7), 493–503.
