Rain, brown noise or silence: finding the sound you focus with

A Sidenote study guide · Published November 21, 2026

Ask ten students what they listen to while studying and you get ten answers: rain, lo-fi, a café recording, brown noise, total silence. Each of them is sure theirs is the right one. They may all be right, because the best background sound differs from person to person.

What the research does and doesn't say

The evidence is mixed, and the most consistent finding is that people differ.

  • Speech and lyrics get in the way of reading. Decades of work on what researchers call the irrelevant speech effect shows that background speech disrupts tasks that rely on verbal memory, even when you try to ignore it. If you are reading or writing, songs with words in a language you understand are the riskiest choice.
  • Steady noise helps some people and hurts others. In a small 2007 study, Göran Söderlund and colleagues found that white noise improved memory performance in children with ADHD and made it worse in children without it. It was a small study of children, and later results have been mixed, so take it as one hint that the same sound can help one person and get in another's way.
  • Sound can also work as a mask. In a noisy dorm or café, a steady background covers sudden sounds (a door, a conversation) that would otherwise pull your attention. Here the noise isn't helping your brain directly. It hides the things that would interrupt you.

We haven't found good evidence that any one colour of noise is best for everyone. Be sceptical of anyone who says otherwise.

The kinds of sound, in plain words

  • White noise has equal energy at every pitch. It sounds like a bright hiss. It masks well and is the harshest to listen to.
  • Pink noise is softer in the high pitches. Steady rain is close to it.
  • Brown noise is deeper still, like a distant waterfall or the inside of a plane.
  • Natural scenes (rain, waves, wind, a fire) are mostly noise with slow changes and occasional events. Some people find the variation calming. Others find themselves listening to it.
  • Rhythmic scenes (a train, crickets) add a pulse. A pulse can steady you, and it can also become the thing you pay attention to.
  • Silence is a real option, and for demanding reading it is often the best one, as long as your surroundings are quiet.

How to find yours: run a small experiment

You can't settle this by thinking about it. You have to test it.

  1. Pick three or four candidates, including silence.
  2. Use one per study block, for the whole block. Don't switch halfway.
  3. Rate your focus right after each block, on the same 1-to-5 scale, before you look at your phone.
  4. Keep the task type similar where you can. Problem sets and reading may have different winners.
  5. Wait for at least three blocks per sound before you believe a difference. One good session proves nothing; you might just have slept well.

Sidenote does this bookkeeping for you. Every block records which scene was playing and the focus score you ended with. After three rated blocks it starts showing which scene you focus best with, pulls small samples toward your average so one lucky session doesn't decide it, and suggests untried scenes with a similar character.

Practical settings

  • Keep it quiet. The sound should sit just above the room, low enough that you forget it is on.
  • Avoid surprises. Playlists with ads or sudden changes in volume defeat the purpose.
  • Protect your hearing. If you study for hours with headphones, keep the volume low.

Source

  • Söderlund, G., Sikström, S., & Smart, A. (2007). Listen to the noise: Noise is beneficial for cognitive performance in ADHD. Journal of Child Psychology and Psychiatry, 48(8), 840–847.
