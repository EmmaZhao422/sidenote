Planning an exam week in study blocks

A Sidenote study guide · Published November 21, 2026

A week before exams, most plans look like this: a list of chapters and a hope. This guide swaps the list for a count of study blocks, because blocks are what you actually have. It takes about twenty minutes with a sheet of paper.

Step 1: count your blocks

Go through the week day by day and write down how many study blocks each day can really hold. Count classes, meals, travel, sleep and one thing you enjoy. A block here means 25 to 45 minutes of work plus a short break. Most people find the total is smaller than they assumed, and it is better to find that out on day one.

Step 2: assume it takes longer

People underestimate how long their own work will take. In a well-known 1994 study by Roger Buehler, Dale Griffin and Michael Ross, 37 students predicted when they would finish their thesis. The average prediction was about 34 days. The average result was about 56, and only around three in ten finished by the date they had named. In the same paper, people were more realistic when they predicted how long someone else would take.

Two habits follow from that:

  • Estimate from your record. Ask how long this kind of task took last time. If one problem set took three blocks, the next one takes three, however fast you feel today.
  • Leave a fifth of the week empty. If you have 20 blocks, plan 16. The other four are for whatever overruns. If nothing does, you have four blocks of extra practice.

Step 3: spread each subject out

Give every subject several short visits across the week instead of one long day. A large 2013 review of study techniques by John Dunlosky and colleagues rated this, distributed practice, as one of the two most useful things a student can do. Coming back after a gap forces you to pull the material up again, and that is what makes it stay.

A simple pattern for three subjects over six days is A B C A B C, with two or three blocks each day, and the last day kept for the exam that comes first.

Step 4: make most blocks a test

The other technique that review rated highest is practice testing. In a 2006 experiment by Henry Roediger and Jeffrey Karpicke, students either read a passage four times, or read it once and then tried three times to write down what they remembered. Five minutes later the re-readers did better. A week later the picture had reversed: the group that tested itself recalled about 61% of the passage, and the group that only re-read recalled about 40%.

An exam week is that one-week gap. So plan blocks as tests:

  • Past papers and end-of-chapter questions, done with the book closed.
  • A blank page: write everything you know about a topic, then check what you missed.
  • Flashcards, answering before you turn the card over.

Keep re-reading for the parts a test showed you don't know. Our guide to self-testing has the routine for a single block.

Step 5: fix tomorrow's first block tonight

At the end of each day, write down the first block of the next day: the subject, the exact task, and the time you will start. Plans that name a time and a first action get acted on more often than plans that name only a goal. It also means you start with the work, and skip the half hour of deciding what to do.

During the week

  • Exam worries will show up in the middle of blocks. Write each one down in a line and keep going. The guide to worry covers what to do with the list afterwards.
  • Keep your breaks. Skipping them looks like more study time and usually produces less. See what to do in a break.
  • The night before, one light block of self-testing on the main ideas, then stop and sleep. Check the room, the time and what you need to bring before you go to bed, so that none of it is on your mind in the morning.

Where Sidenote fits

Set the exam date on the home page and it counts down the days. The summary page shows how many minutes went to each goal, so by midweek you can see which subject is short of blocks. And "Remind me tomorrow" at the end of a block puts the next one in your calendar.
