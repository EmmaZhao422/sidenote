Where should your phone be while you study?

A Sidenote study guide

You have probably heard that a phone on your desk makes you think worse even if you never touch it. The claim comes from a real study. Later research has complicated it, and the practical advice turns out to be simpler than the headline.

The original claim

In 2017, Adrian Ward and colleagues reported what they called the brain drain effect. People did tests of working memory and reasoning with their phone on the desk, in a pocket or bag, or in another room. Those with the phone in another room did best, even though nobody was using their phone. The authors' explanation was that part of your attention is spent on not checking it.

What happened next

Other labs tried to repeat the result, and it has not held up cleanly.

  • A 2022 replication by Ruiz Pardo and Minda used the same tasks and found no difference between phone locations.
  • A 2023 meta-analysis by Böttger, Poschik and Zierer found a significant negative effect overall when it pooled studies of phone use and phone presence together.
  • A 2024 meta-analysis by Hartanto and colleagues looked only at mere presence, across 33 studies and 4,368 participants, and found no significant effect.
  • Another 2024 meta-analysis, by Douglas Parry, found a small negative effect on working memory and nothing for other abilities, and called the support limited.

So a silent phone lying face down may cost you a little, or nothing. The evidence is mixed, and anyone who tells you it is settled is overselling it.

What matters in practice

The lab studies test a phone that stays silent and untouched. That isn't the usual problem when you study. The usual problem is that the phone lights up, or you pick it up "for a second", and the block is gone. You don't need a study to know what the next twenty minutes look like after that.

Whatever the mere-presence effect turns out to be, the useful move is the same: make the phone harder to reach than the thing you are studying.

  1. Put it out of arm's reach, ideally out of sight: a bag across the room, a shelf behind you, another room if you can.
  2. Silence it properly. Vibration on a desk is still an interruption. Use Do Not Disturb, and allow calls from the two or three people who might really need you.
  3. Give the urge somewhere to go. When you think "I should check if she replied", write that down in one line and keep going. The thought is safe, and you can check when the block ends. This is the parking lot method.
  4. If your phone is your timer, use a timer on your computer instead, so the phone has no reason to be on the desk.

In Sidenote, urges get their own category. If "check my phone" keeps showing up in your parked thoughts, the summary page will show it, along with the minute of the block when it tends to hit.
