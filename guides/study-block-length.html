25 minutes or 45? How long a study block should be

A Sidenote study guide · Published November 21, 2026

The Pomodoro technique says 25 minutes of work, then a 5-minute break. Other people swear by 50 and 10, or by working until they feel like stopping. So which one is right?

Researchers have only recently started testing this with students doing their real coursework, and so far the answer is: no single number wins. What the studies do show is useful for choosing your own.

What the studies found

In 2023, Felicitas Biwer and colleagues at Maastricht University followed students through a day of their own studying. One group decided for themselves when to take breaks. Two other groups took breaks on a fixed schedule: 6 minutes after every 24 minutes, or 3 minutes after every 12.

  • Students who chose their own breaks studied in longer stretches and also took longer breaks.
  • The groups didn't differ in how much mental effort they reported or in how much of their work they completed.
  • Fixed, pre-planned breaks came with better mood, and appeared to be more efficient: similar work in less time.

A follow-up study from the same university in 2025 compared self-chosen breaks, Pomodoro breaks (25 and 5) and a third method. This time fatigue rose faster in the Pomodoro group, and again the groups ended up without overall differences.

So the evidence doesn't crown 25 minutes. It suggests that deciding your block length in advance is what helps, because you stop spending attention on the question "should I take a break now?"

How to choose your length

  1. Start with 25 minutes. It is short enough to begin on a bad day.
  2. Look at when your mind starts to wander. If your stray thoughts cluster around minute 15, a 45-minute block is mostly a fight. If you are still steady at minute 25 and annoyed when the timer rings, go longer.
  3. Match the block to the task. Flashcards and problem drills suit short blocks. Writing and long proofs need time to load into your head, so 45 or 60 minutes often works better.
  4. Change one thing at a time and give it a few sessions before judging.

Sidenote's summary page has a chart called "When in a block your mind wandered". It counts your parked thoughts in five-minute stretches. If the bars pile up late in the block, shorten it. If they pile up in the first five minutes, the block length isn't your problem and the start is; see the five-minute start.

Breaks matter as much as blocks

A break only works if it actually rests the part of you that was working. Stand up, look at something far away, get water. Scrolling a feed isn't rest for your attention, and it makes the next start harder. If you want to check your phone, that is exactly the kind of urge to park and deal with after the last block of the day.
