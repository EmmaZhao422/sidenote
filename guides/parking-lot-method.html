The parking lot method: what to do with a thought that shows up mid-study

A Sidenote study guide · Published November 21, 2026

You sit down to study. Eight minutes in, your brain reminds you that you never replied to that email. Then it wonders why the sky is blue. Then it asks whether you are behind everyone else in the class. None of these thoughts is urgent, and each one feels like it needs attention right now.

You can't stop thoughts from showing up. What you can change is what happens next. The parking lot method is one rule: write the thought down in one line, and go back to work. You deal with it later, at a time you choose.

Why a stray thought is so hard to ignore

An unfinished task nags. Psychologists have studied this since the 1920s, when Bluma Zeigarnik reported that people remembered interrupted tasks better than completed ones. Later studies of that specific memory effect have been mixed, but the everyday experience behind it is familiar: an open loop keeps asking for attention.

A more useful finding came in 2011. E. J. Masicampo and Roy Baumeister found that unfinished goals intruded on people's thinking during an unrelated task, and that the intrusions largely went away once people made a specific plan for the unfinished goal. They didn't have to finish it. Deciding when and how they would handle it was enough.

Parking a thought is probably doing something similar. Those experiments tested plans that said when and how, so the closer your note comes to that (one line now, a set time to deal with it later), the closer you are to what the participants did.

How to do it

  1. Keep one capture spot open before you start. A sheet of paper beside your notes, or the box at the bottom of a Sidenote study block. One spot only. If you have to decide where to write, you have already left your work.
  2. Write one line, no more. "Email Prof. Lin re: extension." "Why is the sky blue?" You are leaving a pointer to the thought, and a pointer is all you need.
  3. Don't act on it. No new tab, no phone, no "this will only take a second." The line you wrote is the action.
  4. Go back to the exact sentence you were on. Put your finger or cursor on it if that helps.
  5. Empty the lot when the block ends. This step is what makes the method work. If parked thoughts are never dealt with, your brain learns that the list is a place where things get lost, and it stops letting go.

Emptying the lot

Go through the list once and give every line one of four endings:

  • Answer it. Questions you were curious about. Look them up now, when it costs you nothing.
  • Schedule it. Errands and to-dos get a time, or a first step that takes under two minutes.
  • Talk it through. Worries often shrink when you look at them outside of study time. Ask what part of it you can act on.
  • Drop it. Plenty of mid-study thoughts turn out to be nothing. Cross them off.

Francesco Cirillo's Pomodoro Technique has a version of this idea: when an internal interruption comes up during a 25-minute session, you mark it, note it down, and carry on. The parking lot method takes that note seriously afterwards.

What the list tells you over time

After a week, read back over what you parked. Patterns show up quickly. If most lines are errands, you may need a five-minute admin slot before you study. If most are worries about one course, that points to a conversation with a tutor. If they cluster around minute fifteen, your blocks may be a little too long. Sidenote keeps this record for you and shows it on the summary page.

Common ways it goes wrong

  • Writing paragraphs. If capturing takes more than ten seconds, you have switched tasks.
  • Several lists. Phone notes, sticky notes and a notebook all at once means none of them is trusted.
  • Never reviewing. The method only works if the lot gets emptied.
