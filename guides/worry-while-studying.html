A worry showed up while you were studying. Give it an appointment.

A Sidenote study guide · Published November 21, 2026

Errands and questions are easy to park. You write "buy printer paper" and it stays written. Worries are harder. "What if I fail this course" does not sit quietly on a list. It comes back two minutes later in slightly different words.

You don't need to solve the worry in order to study. You need it to wait. One way to get that is to give it a time.

The idea: postpone, don't suppress

In 1983 Thomas Borkovec and colleagues tried something simple with people who described themselves as worriers. For four weeks, participants were asked to notice when they started worrying, postpone it, and do their worrying in one set half-hour each day, at the same time and place. The approach is called stimulus control, or worry postponement, and it is still used as one part of cognitive behavioural treatment for worry.

The key word is postpone. Telling yourself "don't think about it" tends to fail. Telling yourself "not now, at six" is a deal your mind can accept, as long as you keep your side of it.

How to do it during a study block

  1. Write the worry in one line, as it came. "What if I fail the midterm." Don't tidy it up or argue with it.
  2. Tell it when. "After this block" is enough. If it is a big one, pick a time today.
  3. Go back to the sentence you were on. If it returns, you don't need to write it again. It is already on the list.
  4. Keep the appointment. When the block ends, read the line again and ask two questions. Is there anything I can do about this? If yes, what is the smallest next step, and when will I do it? If no, say so in writing: "nothing to do about this today."

What the appointment usually shows

Looked at after the block, most study-time worries fall into one of three groups.

  • A task in disguise. "I'm so behind in stats" turns into "do problem set 4 tomorrow at ten." Once it has a time, treat it like any other to-do.
  • A question for a person. "Is it too late to switch majors?" has a factual answer that an advisor knows. The next step is an email.
  • Something you can't act on today. Results that aren't out yet, what someone thinks of you. Naming it as "not actionable today" is often enough for now.

What the research does and doesn't show

The evidence for worry postponement is real but mixed. The early studies and several later ones found that people who postponed spent less time worrying. A large online trial that simply sent people instructions found no benefit. A fair summary: it is a cheap thing to try, it works better when you actually hold the later appointment, and it is no cure.

This guide is about ordinary study worries. If worry is constant, keeps you from sleeping, or comes with a feeling that you can't cope, that is a reason to talk to a counsellor or doctor. Most universities have a free service. If you are thinking about harming yourself, contact a local emergency number or crisis line now.
