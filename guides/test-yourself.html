Re-reading feels like studying. Testing yourself works better.

A Sidenote study guide · Published November 21, 2026

A timer and a quiet room get you to the desk. What you do while the timer runs matters at least as much. Many students spend that time re-reading and highlighting, and the research on those two habits is not kind.

What the review found

In 2013 John Dunlosky and four colleagues published a long review of ten study techniques that students can use on their own. They rated each one by how well it held up across ages, subjects and kinds of test.

  • Rated high: practice testing (quizzing yourself) and distributed practice (spreading study over several sessions).
  • Rated low: highlighting or underlining, re-reading, summarising, the keyword mnemonic, and forming mental images of text.

Low doesn't mean useless. It means the benefit is small or shows up only in narrow conditions. The trouble with re-reading is that the page looks more familiar each time, and familiarity is easy to mistake for knowing. A test tells you what you can actually produce from memory.

How to self-test in one block

  1. Read a section once, properly. Then close the book or switch the tab.
  2. Write down everything you can recall, or answer the questions at the end of the section, without looking. Half a page of messy notes is fine.
  3. Open the book and check. Mark what you missed or got wrong. Those marks are your study list.
  4. Re-study only the marked parts, then test those again before the block ends.

For problem-based subjects, the test is already there: cover the worked solution and solve the problem cold. For subjects with a lot of facts, flashcards do the same job, as long as you answer before you turn the card over.

Spread it out

The other high-rated technique costs nothing. For the same total time, three 25-minute blocks on three different days usually do better than one 75-minute block the night before, especially when the test is days or weeks away. The gap between sessions lets you forget a little, and pulling the material back is what strengthens it. If you plan a week, give each subject several short slots.

Why it feels worse

Self-testing is uncomfortable, because it keeps showing you what you don't know. Re-reading is pleasant, because nothing pushes back. That discomfort is useful. It is also when stray thoughts are most tempting, since any other thought is a way out. Expect them, park them in one line, and go back to the question you were stuck on.
